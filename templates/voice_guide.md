# Voice Guide: The Digital Toolkit

This is the publication's voice: a calm, practical technology guide. It is not a made-up person. Never invent an author, credentials, personal experience or product testing.

## Clear English — non-negotiable
- Write for an international reader who may be new to the subject. Aim for US grade 7–8.
- Use common words. Prefer "use", "help", "start", "show" and "buy" over inflated alternatives.
- Keep most sentences near 15 words. Split long sentences. Avoid sentences over 28 words.
- Keep paragraphs short, usually 1–3 sentences. Explain one idea at a time.
- Explain technical terms briefly the first time they appear. Spell out an acronym once.
- Prefer active voice, concrete examples and direct instructions.
- Do not use slang, hype, fake quotes or fake statistics. Never say you or we tested a product unless the publication actually did so.
- Avoid filler openings, repeated conclusions and phrases that merely restate the heading.

## The eight editorial categories
Choose the article's main category based on the reader's problem and intended outcome, not merely the tool mentioned. Use the category supplied in the article brief; do not silently rename it or drift into another category.

### AI and AI Tools
- Explain what the AI tool can and cannot do for the specific task.
- Distinguish the model's capability from the app interface, account plan, usage limits and connected services.
- Include a practical way to verify important AI-generated facts.
- Check privacy, data use, free-versus-paid limits and regional availability when relevant.

### Office Software and Everyday Productivity
- Name the application and platform. Give menu names or interface paths only when they are known.
- Do not assume Microsoft Office and Google Workspace features are identical.
- In tutorials, explain what the reader should see after each important step and how to confirm success.
- Use a complete, small example when it makes a document, calendar or workflow easier to reproduce.

### Spreadsheets, Data and Reporting
- For formula tutorials, show a small, complete sample dataset and identify the exact cell locations.
- Show the exact formula, the cell where the reader enters it, and the expected calculated output.
- Independently recalculate every worked example. Check cell references, ranges, criteria, blank cells, and boundary cases.
- Explain what each formula does and distinguish similar functions such as AVERAGE and AVERAGEIF.
- Do not leave a formula displayed as text when the article promises calculated results. Give the expected value for each example row or state clearly when the result depends on reader data.
- Distinguish Excel-specific features from Google Sheets features. Explain compatibility or version differences when they matter.
- For charts and reports, state what data the chart uses and what the reader should learn from it.

### Graphic Design, Video and Content Creation
- Distinguish genuine product screenshots from illustrative artwork or mockups.
- State dimensions, aspect ratios, export formats and platform constraints accurately when relevant.
- Explain licensing and usage rights for fonts, stock images, music, templates and generated media when relevant.
- For short-form video, use the requested destination format consistently; do not mix portrait and landscape framing in one workflow unless explicitly required.

### Freelancing and Remote Work
- Label invented client examples as hypothetical. Keep scope, deliverables, timeline, assumptions and any illustrative figures consistent.
- Treat rates, deposits, milestones and payment arrangements as negotiable, not universal rules.
- Verify country support and withdrawal conditions before recommending a payment service.
- Do not promise clients, income, conversion rates or guaranteed results.

### Digital Business, Marketing and E-commerce
- Distinguish platform features, eligibility, plan limits, transaction fees and payout conditions.
- Explain affiliate disclosures when recommending affiliate products or links.
- Avoid guaranteed income, traffic, conversion or sales claims.
- Verify payment, checkout and payout availability for the reader's country before making regional claims.

### Digital Learning, Research and Study Skills
- Distinguish learning support from completing assessed work dishonestly.
- Show readers how to evaluate source credibility and verify AI-generated research.
- Never invent citations, academic references, quotations or research findings.
- Make study workflows realistic and usable, rather than promising grades or outcomes.

### Digital Foundations, Online Safety and Troubleshooting
- Give safe, reversible diagnostic steps before destructive actions such as resets or deletion.
- Describe the symptom, likely causes, exact steps and expected result.
- Never ask readers to reveal passwords, recovery codes, private keys or sensitive account details.
- Distinguish general security advice from instructions that depend on a specific device, operating system or service.

## Choose the right article format
Match the structure to the reader's intent. Do not force every article into a generic tutorial.
- Beginner guide: explain core terms, prerequisites and a sensible first step.
- Step-by-step tutorial: give ordered actions, expected screen or output, and a success check.
- Practical project: define the finished deliverable, inputs, steps and completed example.
- Formula or calculation tutorial: show inputs, exact formula, formula location, calculated outputs and an independent check.
- Troubleshooting guide: describe symptoms, likely causes, safe diagnostic steps and a verifiable fix.
- Comparison: use consistent criteria, distinguish verified facts from judgment, and explain which option suits which reader.
- Review or evaluation: disclose limitations and never imply hands-on testing that did not happen.
- Explainer: explain how something works, why it matters and where its limits are.
- Checklist or template: include the complete promised checklist or usable template.

## Write like an article, not a directory
- Build a connected explanation with a clear beginning, middle and end.
- Use paragraphs to explain why something matters, how it works and what the reader should do.
- Do not turn every paragraph into a list, card, tool profile or feature inventory.
- Do not add tool names, alternatives, links or headings just to make the article look longer.
- Use a list only when it makes steps, choices or details easier to scan.
- Use comparison tables only when the reader genuinely needs to compare options.
- For a tutorial, guide the reader through the task in order. Explain what they should see and why each step matters.
- For a single-tool article, stay focused on the reader's task. Do not pad it with unrelated alternatives.
- Vary article structure naturally. Do not force every article into the same set of headings or FAQ.

## Helpful and honest
- Answer the main question near the start.
- Include useful detail, examples, limits, common mistakes and next steps when relevant.
- Never claim that the publication tested or used a tool. Attribute details to official pages or verified sources.
- Say when a detail could not be confirmed. Never guess prices, limits, availability or features.
- Explain who a tool may not suit, where that helps the reader decide.
- Mention a free plan or trial accurately; explain important limits.

## Links and references
- Link to a source in the body only where it supports a specific statement or helps the reader take an action.
- Avoid repeated links to the same page. Do not link every mention of a product or keyword.
- Do not create a directory of external resources.
- Include exactly one final "Sources & References" section, at the very bottom, only when sources are cited. Never create both "Sources", "References", "Sources & Resources" and similar sections.
- The final section should contain only the sources actually used. Each source appears once. No duplicate list elsewhere.

## Screenshots and images
- Use the featured image as the article's main visual.
- Add screenshots only when they help the reader understand a real step, screen, setting or result.
- Place each screenshot immediately after the paragraph or step it explains, not in a gallery at the bottom.
- Use as many screenshots as the task needs; do not force a quota. A detailed walkthrough may need several, while an opinion or explainer may need none.
- Use genuine screenshots from the real product or official page. Never invent a user interface or imply a generated mockup is a real screenshot.
- Give every image useful alt text and a short caption that explains what the reader should notice.
